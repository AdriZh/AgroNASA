Question 1 · Does the idea fit?

Yes, once the app advises on rotations rather than rating soil

The first idea was a map that tells a farmer which soil is better. Challenge 6 asks for something else. It wants a decision tool that compares at least three crop-rotation strategies for a field, explains each one, and leaves the final call to the farmer. The evaluation report gets this right. Its re-aim (outline a field, build a field profile from NASA data, compare three rotations with an evidence card each) answers all ten asks in the brief. We agree with it, with three changes for a two-day build.

Change 1 · narrow the place

Build for one region, the northern Kazakh grain belt, where the team knows the farming. The analysis below shows its rotation rules differ sharply from the US advice most tools are built on.

Change 2 · cut the stack

Skip the on-phone language model, PMTiles and Capacitor for the weekend. Ship POWER weather, SoilGrids, a crop table, the water model and the evidence cards. Add greenness (HLS or MODIS) only if Earthdata login works by Saturday evening.

Change 3 · lead with drought risk

Rank rotations first on how often they fail in a dry year, using the 25-year replay. That is the number a steppe farmer cares about most, and few other teams will show it.

Question 2 · What problem are we solving?

Farmers choose rotations by habit and price, without seeing their own field's water and soil limits

Problem statement

A grain farmer in a dry region has no simple way to see how different rotations would have done on their own field across the last 25 dry and wet years. So the field stays in short, wheat-heavy rotations that drain soil carbon and water and leave the soil bare for months, and generic advice such as "plant a cover crop" often fails there.

Cause · economics

Markets reward one crop

Wheat dominates the northern Kazakh grain belt. Across the US, about 18% of corn acres were continuous corn in 2010, and maize–soy dominates the rest. Short rotations raise pest, disease and nutrient losses.

Cause · information

Data exists but is not usable

NASA records weather, soil moisture and greenness, but as gridded files at different scales. A farmer cannot turn them into "what happens if I swap wheat for lentil".

Cause · wrong advice

Rules don't transfer

Cover crops cut next-crop yields where annual rain is below about 700 mm (Garba et al. 2022). Our pilot gets about 400 mm. Advice built for Iowa backfires on the steppe.

Effect · soil

Degradation compounds

About a third of soils are moderately to highly degraded (FAO 2015). Over 60% of human-induced degradation is on farmland (FAO SOLAW 2025). Bare, dry steppe fields lose topsoil to wind.

Effect · risk

Drought years wipe out income

In our model, rain-fed spring wheat at the pilot lost more than half its water-limited yield in 10 of 25 years. Diverse rotations cut drought-year maize losses by 14–90% in long-term trials (Bowles et al. 2020).

Who

The user

A family or mid-size grain farm with patchy mobile data, often advised by an extension agent or agronomist. The tool has to work on a cheap phone and produce a summary they can discuss.

Global figures come from the evaluation report's source table (FAO, UNCCD, USDA ERS and the cited studies). Check each against its source before putting it in the pitch. The per-field figures are our own model output, explained in the method notes at the end.

Question 3 · What does the data say?

Five findings from 25 years of NASA records

Each chart has a hover tooltip and a table view. The pilot is the Akmola steppe. Turkistan (irrigated cotton belt) and central Iowa (where most rotation and cover-crop advice comes from) are included for contrast.

1 · Rain falls far below the line where cover crops pay off

Annual rainfall, 2001–2025. The dashed reference marks the roughly 700 mm break-even for cover crops in Garba et al. (2022). Iowa sits above it almost every year. Both Kazakh sites never reach it, so the app must not suggest a post-harvest cover crop there without a water warning.

NASA POWER PRECTOTCORR (MERRA-2, bias-corrected), daily, summed by year. District-scale (about 50 km pixel).

2 · Water demand exceeds rain in every growing month on the steppe

Average monthly rainfall (bars) against reference evapotranspiration, the water a crop would use if it never ran short (line). Where the line runs above the bars, a crop draws on stored soil water and then goes short. In Akmola that gap runs from May to August, the whole wheat season. Snowmelt water stored in spring is the farmer's main reserve, and rotation choices either protect it or use it up.

After harvest, the window for a cover crop is short and dry

Days from the main crop's typical harvest to the first hard frost (below −2 °C), and the heat and rain in that window. Median of 25 years. In Akmola about 35 mm of rain falls in that window, against about 95 mm of evaporative demand, so a cover crop there mostly uses up water the next crop needs.

Harvest date = regional sowing date + FAO-56 season length. GDD base 4 °C. Rain and ET₀ from POWER.

What is changing: 25-year trends

Theil–Sen slope per decade with a Mann–Kendall test. Only trends with p < 0.05 are marked significant. Most changes at the pilot are not yet significant, so year-to-year swings matter more than the trend for rotation choice. In Turkistan the growing season is warming, by about 0.6 °C a decade.

What this means for the product

Five design rules the data gives us

Finding 1 → rule

Rain decides which advice is valid

Run a water check before any rotation reaches the screen. Below 700 mm a year, mark cover crops "uses stored water" and prefer pulses or oilseeds that fit inside the season.

Finding 2 → rule

Show the water budget per crop

Each strategy card shows the crop's water need against normal and dry-year rain, from POWER and FAO-56. This is the evidence the brief asks for.

Finding 3 → rule

Rank on bad years, not average years

Score "risk" as the share of the 25 seasons below 0.5. At the pilot, chickpea and sunflower fail far less often than wheat or pea under the same weather.

Finding 4 → rule

Replay every year

Show the spread of outcomes as dots, not one number. At the pilot the worst seasons were 2004, 2008 and 2010; farmers will recognise their own drought years, which builds trust.

Finding 5 → rule

Tag every number by origin

Label each value: measured on the field, district-scale model, soil-map estimate, or entered by the farmer. Show soil as a range, and let a soil test replace it.

The three rotations the pilot app would compare (draft for agronomist review)

  1. Current: wheat – wheat – wheat (or wheat with summer fallow). The baseline every farmer knows.
  2. Small change: wheat – wheat – lentil or pea. A legume break adds nitrogen (the next crop gains about 20% on average, Zhao et al. 2022) and interrupts cereal diseases.
  3. Bigger shift: wheat – chickpea or lentil – canola or flax – wheat. Four families, the lowest dry-year failure rate in our replay, and a wider spread of income sources.
Method, sources and limits

How the numbers were made

Data used

  • NASA POWER daily point API, AG community, 2001–2025: temperature (mean, max, min), corrected rainfall, root-zone and surface soil wetness, solar radiation, humidity, wind. No key needed; about 2 s per site.
  • ISRIC SoilGrids 2.0: SOC, pH, clay, sand, silt, nitrogen and bulk density at five depths, with 5th and 95th percentiles.
  • Not yet used: HLS or MODIS greenness for bare windows and crop calendars. MODIS through the ORNL point service and HLS need an Earthdata login or failed TLS from this sandbox. Next step for the team.

Methods

  • ET₀ by FAO-56 Penman–Monteith; crop ET by FAO-56 Kc curves (4 stages).
  • Daily soil-water bucket; available water from Saxton & Rawls (2006) using SoilGrids texture and carbon. Starting moisture from POWER GWETROOT on the sowing day.
  • Yield response by FAO-33 Ky. Ky for lentil, chickpea, flax and canola is assumed and needs agronomist review.
  • Trends by Theil–Sen slope and Mann–Kendall test.
  • Code: analysis/fetch_data.py and analysis/analyze.py in the team repository.
Known limits of this analysis
  • One point per region. POWER and soil wetness are district-scale (9–50 km), not field-scale.
  • No snow-trapping, no stubble effects, no runoff and no fallow carry-over in the bucket. These favour wheat-fallow systems in reality, so the wheat numbers are conservative.
  • The yield index is water-only. It ignores heat at flowering, frost, pests, nitrogen and prices.
  • POWER rainfall is a reanalysis product. A local rain gauge should correct it in the app.
  • Turkistan crops are irrigated in practice; its rain-fed index shows how much water irrigation has to supply.
